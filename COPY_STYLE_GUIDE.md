# Copy Style Guide

## Voice

Warm, reassuring, clear, clinically responsible and confident without overselling.

## Writing rules

- Start with the patient's real concern or desired outcome.
- Use short sentences when the topic is stressful or technical.
- Explain clinical terms in everyday language.
- Use treatment names for clarity and SEO, not as the emotional headline every time.
- Do not use em dashes.
- Avoid generic phrases such as "world-class", "state-of-the-art", "transform your life", "unparalleled care" and "cutting-edge solutions" unless a specific factual claim supports them.
- Avoid "best dentist", "No. 1", "100% painless", "guaranteed", "permanent forever" and similar claims.
- Do not invent qualifications, memberships, technologies, awards, patient counts or case counts.
- Never imply that a website page can diagnose a patient.

## Core brand language

Useful themes:

- comfort
- clarity
- confidence
- relief
- trust
- care
- precision
- restore
- understand your options
- feel comfortable asking questions

## CTA language

Prefer:

- Book a consultation
- Ask us on WhatsApp
- Tell us what is bothering you
- Explore your options
- Find a branch
- Plan an international enquiry

Avoid using "Book Now" everywhere.

## Positioning, September 2026

**The one-line position.** Most dental sites in this market sell painlessness and
superlatives. Kheni sells the thing patients actually cannot get elsewhere: a
straight answer. Every headline, every FAQ and every CTA either delivers a
straight answer or promises one.

Tagline: **Every smile starts with a straight answer.**

## The four silent questions

Research into dental website conversion is consistent on this: before a patient
picks up the phone they are privately answering four questions. Copy that ignores
any of them leaks leads. Every page should answer at least one, and the homepage
answers all four above the fold or close to it.

| The question they are asking | How we answer it |
| --- | --- |
| Will it hurt? | Local anaesthesia, and we say what you will feel before it happens rather than after. Say you are nervous and the pace changes. Never "painless". |
| Will I be judged? | "It has been a while. You will not get a lecture." Said plainly, on the homepage, in the concern finder. |
| Can I afford it? | No prices on the site, because cost follows the examination. The plan and the estimate come before treatment, and staged work is agreed one stage at a time. |
| Is it worth my time? | Honest timelines. A filling is one visit. Implants run over months because bone heals on its own schedule. |

## Audience notes

**The avoider.** Has not been in years, knows it, expects a telling off. Cost is
cited more often than fear as the reason for staying away, so both get answered.
Lead with permission, not urgency.

**The parent.** Worried less about the cavity than about their child being
frightened for life. Speak to the parent about the child's experience, not about
paediatric dentistry.

**The implant or full mouth case.** Has usually been quoted somewhere else and is
trying to work out who to trust. Wants to know what gets checked, in what order,
and what happens if the answer is no. Specificity beats reassurance.

**The NRI.** Has a fixed number of days in India and is terrified of starting
something that cannot be finished. Tell them what fits in the trip and what does
not, before they book flights.

## Writing moves that work here

- Put the patient's own sentence in their mouth. "A gap you have learned to chew
  around" beats "missing tooth replacement".
- Use the ordinary detail. "Pink in the sink" is how people actually describe
  bleeding gums.
- Answer the objection in the headline, not in a footnote.
- One highlighted phrase per line, set in rose by `hl` or the `highlight` prop.
  It should land on the emotional word, not the clinical one.
- Punchy does not mean superlative. "Chew on both sides again" is punchier than
  anything with "best" in it, and it is also true.

## Lines that are off limits, and why

The content integrity check fails the build on these, so this is enforced rather
than advisory: superlatives (best, No. 1, leading, world class, state of the art,
cutting edge, perfect smile), any success percentage, "painless", "guaranteed",
"happy patients", "award", "under one roof", "teeth in a day", em dashes, and
services the clinic did not confirm (sedation, laser gum treatment, airport
pickup, visa letters, round-the-clock support).

Most category-standard dental slogans fail this list. That is the point. The
constraint is what makes the voice distinctive.
